Why School Leaders Drift from Their Goals — And What Actually Helps

Vanna O'Conner Founder & Executive Director, CenterED Education

Most principals I know start the year with real clarity. They've spent the summer thinking hard about their school, their students, and what needs to change. They come back in August with a plan, a vision, and genuine energy to make it happen.

And then October hits.

By the time winter break arrives, many of those same leaders are running on fumes, putting out fires, and quietly wondering what happened to the work they committed to in September. The plan didn't disappear. It just got buried under everything else.

This isn't a willpower problem. And it's not a planning problem either. In fact, most schools already know how to write strong plans. What they struggle with is something harder to name: the ongoing work of staying aligned, staying focused, and adjusting the plan in real time when reality doesn't cooperate.

The real culprit isn't urgency. It's isolation.

When I talk to school leaders about what makes goal-following so hard, urgency always comes up first. The day-to-day pressures are real. A staff conflict, a parent escalation, a district initiative that lands in your inbox with a two-week deadline — any one of these can pull a leader completely off course.

But the deeper issue I keep hearing is isolation. Leaders at the top of a school have very few people they can think out loud with. They can't always be vulnerable with their staff. They don't want to burden their supervisor. And their peer network, if they have one, is informal and inconsistent.

"If you aspire to be a principal, there's a lot of PD. Once you get to that level, there are only so many people who can support you."

That's a quote from a leader I interviewed during CenterED's early customer discovery work. It stuck with me because it captures something I'd seen firsthand for years. The higher you go in a school system, the more support disappears. But the complexity of your role keeps growing.

Three things that actually help

After 15 years of working alongside school and system leaders, I've found that the leaders who stay on track tend to have three things in common. They're not complicated. But they're also not typical.

A real thinking partner. Not a coach who shows up once a quarter. Not a supervisor who evaluates them. A confidential, consistent thought partner who can help them process decisions in real time, before those decisions become crises. Someone who asks the right questions and helps them see what they're too close to see on their own.

A structure for accountability. Goals don't stay alive because leaders care about them. They stay alive because there's a system to revisit them regularly, track progress honestly, and make intentional adjustments. Without that structure, even the best intentions fade.

Peers who get it. There's something specific that happens when a group of leaders sits in a room together and someone finally names the thing everyone's been quietly struggling with. The relief is immediate. And what follows that relief is usually real learning. Peer community isn't a luxury for school leaders. It's a professional necessity.

What this looks like in practice

One of the leaders I've worked with described it this way: she came into our work together knowing what her school's priorities were. She could recite them clearly. But when we started digging into her weekly schedule and her team's meeting structures, it became obvious that her time and her team's energy weren't actually organized around those priorities.

That's not unusual. It's actually one of the most common leadership gaps I see. There's often a real disconnect between what leaders say their priorities are and where they spend their focus day to day.

The work of coaching is closing that gap. Not by adding more to a leader's plate, but by helping them get more intentional about what's already on it.

"The best leadership teams don't just have a vision. They have the support and systems to execute it."

If your school has a strong plan that keeps losing momentum, the answer probably isn't a better plan. It's better support structures around the people responsible for carrying it out.

That's what CenterED was built to provide. And if you're a leader who's tired of starting strong and finishing scattered, I'd love to talk.

Vanna O'Conner is the Founder and Executive Director of CenterED Education. She has spent 15 years coaching school and system leaders across the country.
