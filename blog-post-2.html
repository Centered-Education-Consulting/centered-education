The 3 Supports Every Principal Needs But Rarely Gets

Vanna O'Conner Founder & Executive Director, CenterED Education

I've spent a significant part of my career sitting across from school leaders in some of the most challenging districts in the country. Oakland. Memphis. Los Angeles. Greeley. And no matter the city, the grade level, or the demographics of the school, I keep hearing versions of the same thing.

"I've had to figure most of this out on my own."

That's not a complaint. School leaders are resilient people. They're used to solving problems with limited resources and even more limited time. But there's something deeply wrong with a system that prepares people extensively to become principals and then largely leaves them alone once they get there.

The school principal role has changed dramatically over the last two decades. What was once a managerial position has become something closer to a CEO role, one that requires instructional expertise, people leadership, change management, political navigation, community relations, data analysis, and strategic planning, often simultaneously, often under pressure.

And yet the support structures for principals haven't kept pace. Most districts offer professional development before leaders enter the role. Once they're in it, formal support becomes sparse, episodic, or focused primarily on accountability rather than growth.

Through years of coaching and customer discovery conversations with school and system leaders, three missing supports come up consistently. They're not complicated. But they're also not common.

1. A confidential thinking partner

Not a supervisor. Not a colleague. Not a mentor who checks in occasionally. A consistent, confidential thought partner who's available when the hard decisions actually happen, not two weeks later in a scheduled check-in. Leaders need someone who can help them think through a difficult personnel situation, a conflict with a district directive, or a decision about a struggling teacher, without worrying about how it might affect their evaluation or their reputation. That kind of space is rare. And its absence is costly.

2. A structure to practice inside real problems

There's a meaningful difference between learning about leadership and practicing it. Most principal development programs focus on the former. They offer frameworks, case studies, and simulations. What leaders actually need is the opportunity to work through their real challenges in real time, with someone alongside them who can help them reflect, adjust, and grow from what's actually happening in their building. Development that's disconnected from the real work rarely changes how leaders lead.

3. A community of peers who understand the role

Leadership at the school level can be surprisingly isolating. You're responsible for everyone in the building, which means you often can't be fully transparent with any of them. A structured peer community changes that. Not a networking event or a conference session, but a consistent group of leaders who meet regularly, share real challenges, and hold each other accountable to growth. When principals are in community with other principals, the learning accelerates. And the isolation lifts.

"Maintaining connections with people in different capacities, people to call on and bounce ideas off of, that's what I needed most and had the hardest time finding."

Why these three things aren't more common

It's not that districts don't want to support their principals. Most do, genuinely. But the traditional levers of support, supervision, professional development workshops, instructional coaches, don't address what I'm describing here. They're built around knowledge transfer and accountability. And those things matter. But they don't replace the kind of relational, ongoing, embedded support that actually changes how a leader leads.

Education is also roughly a decade behind corporate sectors in how it thinks about executive coaching. In most industries, coaching isn't reserved for struggling performers. It's considered a standard part of how high-performing leaders stay high-performing. Education is starting to move in that direction. But we're not there yet.

What changes when leaders have these supports

In my experience, when leaders have access to all three, something shifts. They become more proactive and less reactive. They make fewer decisions from a place of stress and more from a place of clarity. Their teams feel it. And eventually, their students do too.

That's not a small thing. That's the whole point.

If you're a school or district leader who recognizes what's described here, I'd love to talk about what it might look like to build these supports into your work this year.

Vanna O'Conner is the Founder and Executive Director of CenterED Education. She has spent 15 years coaching school and system leaders across the country.
