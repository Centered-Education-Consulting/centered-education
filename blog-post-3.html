Knowing vs. Doing: The Real Leadership Gap in Education

Vanna O'Conner Founder & Executive Director, CenterED Education

Early in my career as a math teacher in Memphis, I learned something that has shaped every piece of work I've done since. Knowing what good teaching looks like and actually doing it consistently are two very different things. I could articulate the principles of differentiated instruction. But putting them into practice under real classroom conditions, with 30 students, competing demands, and limited planning time, was a separate challenge entirely.

That gap between knowing and doing doesn't disappear when educators become leaders. If anything, it widens.

School and system leaders today are extraordinarily well informed. They've attended the trainings. They've read the research. They can speak fluently about instructional leadership, adult development, equity, and organizational change. But when I sit with them in coaching sessions and we look honestly at how their time is spent, what their teams are actually working on, and how their school's priorities translate into daily practice, a different picture often emerges.

The knowledge is there. The execution is where things get complicated.

Why knowledge alone isn't enough

We've built an education system that is very good at preparing leaders with knowledge and very underdeveloped at supporting them in applying it. Leadership preparation programs, district professional development, and certification trainings all operate primarily in the knowledge domain. They tell leaders what effective leadership looks like. They don't spend nearly enough time on the messy, real-time work of actually doing it.

This matters especially for leaders working in schools that serve students who've been historically underserved. In those contexts, the stakes of the knowing-doing gap are highest. When a leader knows their school needs a stronger culture of high expectations but struggles to build the systems and structures to make that real, it's the kids who pay the price. Not immediately. Not visibly. But over time, consistently.

"Training transfers knowledge. Supervision enforces expectations. Neither one reliably improves judgment under pressure."

That's the gap I keep coming back to. Judgment under pressure. The ability to make good decisions when things are hard, when competing priorities are pulling in different directions, and when there's no clear right answer. That kind of leadership judgment doesn't come from a workshop. It develops through practice, reflection, and feedback, ideally with someone alongside you who can help you see what you're too close to see.

What closing the gap actually looks like

I've seen this gap close in real time with leaders I've worked with. And when it does, a few things are almost always true.

There's a consistent structure for reflection. Not a year-end debrief. Not a quarterly check-in. A regular, recurring practice of stepping back from the work and asking honest questions. What did I say my priorities were? What did I actually spend my time on? Where did I get pulled off course, and why? That kind of honest self-examination is difficult to do alone. It's much more productive with a thought partner who can ask the questions you wouldn't think to ask yourself.

The work stays connected to real stakes. The best leadership development I've been part of, as a participant and as a facilitator, has always been tethered to something real. Not a case study. Not a simulation. An actual decision the leader was navigating, an actual team they were trying to align, an actual student population they were trying to serve better. When development is grounded in real work, the learning sticks.

Leaders are in community with other leaders. Something shifts when a group of school leaders starts being honest with each other about the knowing-doing gap in their own work. There's less performance. More real problem solving. And a kind of peer accountability that's different from anything a supervisor can provide. I've watched leaders leave those conversations with specific commitments, new strategies, and a sense of being genuinely supported that's hard to replicate any other way.

A different kind of investment

Districts spend significant resources on curriculum, assessments, and instructional materials. Those investments matter. But the educators responsible for implementing all of it, the principals and system leaders who set the conditions for teaching and learning, often receive the least sustained investment in their own ongoing development.

If we want schools to change in ways that last, we have to invest differently in the people leading them. Not just in what they know. But in their capacity to do the hard, daily work of leadership with clarity, confidence, and consistency.

That's what closing the knowing-doing gap looks like. And it's what CenterED was built to support.

"The best leadership teams don't just have a vision. They have the support and systems to execute it."

If you're a leader who recognizes this gap in yourself or your team, I'd love to connect. The work of closing it is some of the most important work we can do for the students who need us most.

Vanna O'Conner is the Founder and Executive Director of CenterED Education. She has spent 15 years coaching school and system leaders across the country.
